# ERC Skill: What Successful Grantees Say

Lessons from talks by ERC grantees, distilled from slide screenshots the PI
collected in October 2026 (`draft/screenshots.docx`), with the PI's own notes
on them. They apply to StG, CoG, and AdG applications. For the evaluation
rules themselves, see `proposal-skills.md` §1 and §10; this file adds what the
grantees found decisive.

## The idea

- **Start with the scientific problem, not the topic or the technology.** A
  compelling ERC question names a problem ("How could pre-industrial cities
  persist despite disease, nutritional stress and demographic instability?"),
  then a unique opportunity to answer it (a 1,800-year urban record in
  Thessaloniki), then makes the ambition explicit (from describing past health
  to explaining the mechanisms of adaptation and persistence).
- **Showcase the question, not only the technology.** A method or system is
  the means; the panel funds the question it answers.
- **Make the idea big but simple.** A conceptually novel idea, ideally grown
  out of the applicant's earlier (interdisciplinary) work, told as a
  straightforward story that is not overly complex.
- **State a clear and strong hypothesis.** Answer the PI's test question:
  *What is the point? What do you expect to find?*
- **Claim a substantial contribution to knowledge, and locate it convincingly
  in the field** so that the panel sees expertise as well as ambition.
- **Show topicality**: rising scientific and societal relevance and urgency.
- **Show the feasibility of the whole and the risk.** Present a clear vision of
  how it will all work and a mitigation strategy if things go badly; but there
  must be real risk, or the project is not ERC material.

## Confidence

- **Be confident, not shy.** Name the breakthrough, the new research pathways,
  and the foundation the project lays for future research.
- **Own the contribution in the text:** "my novel approach", "the model I have
  developed", new concepts. Reviewers credit what the applicant claims
  explicitly.
- **Take a broad perspective**: connect the research to its history, to other
  countries, and to wider processes.

## Structure and presentation

- **Explain the breakthrough before the work packages.** A first-attempt
  grantee overestimated how much methodological detail evaluators need before
  they understand the central breakthrough.
- **Make the central idea understandable to non-specialists**, and give value
  to every discipline involved, so that no panel member feels their field is
  decorative.
- **Use visual representations.** One grantee mapped each research question to
  its methods in a single figure (question -> research question -> data and
  method), which shows the logic of the project at a glance.
- **Budget time for simplification.** Turning a complex interdisciplinary idea
  into a clear, coherent narrative takes longer than expected, as does
  coordinating expertise, work packages, feasibility, and collaborators.

## Team and setup

- **A well-planned team with clearly defined roles** (PhD students, postdocs),
  for example one postdoc and one doctoral student per case study, with
  diverse disciplines and methodological expertise.
- **An advisory board of experts** adds credibility and impact, and can serve
  as the project's ethics board.

## The panel

- **Choose the panel whose evaluators will understand and appreciate the
  idea**, and get to know their work: their disciplines, methods, research
  topics, and countries.
- **Anticipate them:** which questions they might ask, and which parts of the
  proposal they might find unclear or unconvincing.

## The CV

- **Single- or first-authored publications in good venues; the total number
  matters less.** Name the specific publications that relate to the proposal,
  and explain the skills and experience each demonstrates.
- **Show** experience managing (international) projects; scientific, technical,
  and methodological skills; signs of excellence or its potential (awards,
  competitive fellowships, invited talks, positions of trust, important
  collaborations); and unique knowledge or experience directly relevant to the
  topic.

## Process

- **Discuss the idea with others as early as possible**, and pitch it to people
  outside the field.
- **Seek critical feedback and rehearse the story** until it holds up.

## Interview

- **Prepare for the questions more than for the talk.** The 5-minute
  presentation matters less than the 25 minutes of questions; one grantee wrote
  about 50 pages of expected questions with concise answers.
- **Use the presentation strategically:** clarify the key points, convey the
  bigger picture, address gaps or under-explained aspects of the proposal, and
  pre-empt likely criticism.
- **Run mock panels that mirror the expected panel**, staffed by external
  experts rather than friends and colleagues, and repeat them, even with only
  one or two people; grantees accepted being grilled at 6 a.m. for this.
- **Prepare an elevator pitch**, which also tests whether the applicant
  understands the project.
- **During the interview:**
  - be up to date on the latest publications, including those that appeared
    after submission;
  - do not talk too much, and avoid detail that invites further questions;
  - make sure to answer the question asked ("So you are asking me ...?",
    "Could you rephrase the question?", "Does that answer your question?");
  - do not argue or confront, and remember that the panel member may not be an
    expert in the field;
  - practise spoken English until it is fluent.
