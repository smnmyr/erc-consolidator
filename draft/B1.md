## Section b — Extended synopsis (max 5 pages)

### b.1 The problem and the idea (~1.5 pages)

<!-- was: b.1 paragraphs 1-4 before the PI rewrite in B1.docx, 2026-10-29; full text in draft/archive/B1-b1-before-PI-edit-2026-10-29.md -->

The World Wide Web's original design favored simplicity,
decentralization, and low coordination overhead, which enabled
explosive adoption but sacrificed stable references, built-in
versioning, and native bidirectional linking. This was a deliberate
choice: Berners-Lee's 1989 proposal required that the system "allow
existing systems to be linked together without requiring any central
control or coordination" [1], and Fielding and Taylor later clarified
the trade-offs that the resulting architecture yields when defining
the Representational State Transfer (REST) architectural style:
scalable component interactions, uniform interfaces, independently
deployable components, and intermediaries that can be inserted
anywhere [2]. These priorities suited early document-sharing systems
and enabled the Web’s scaling up to billions of users and devices
within three decades. We argue that the Web’s trade-offs are however
too rigid for today’s use cases, where publishers require traceable
dereferencing, supply chain management requires aggregation over
confidential data, courts and parliaments require stable, specific,
citations, users require sovereignty over their data, and society
requires trusted content and accountable authorship.

Over the past twenty years, we have kept the Web’s architecture
fundamentally static and such properties lacking; however, platforms
have responded: Accounts, persistent storage, search, reputation,
payment, and a consistent shared state do not exist between
independent Web sites; platforms provide them inside proprietary
stores into which they draw the Web's content, users, and interactions
[3, 4, 5], and they extend into the rest of the Web through APIs and
plug-ins on their own terms [6]. Users then rebuild continuity
themselves through reposts, screenshots, and off-platform link hubs
[7], yielding unsatisfactory mitigation. Berners-Lee warned in 2010
that social networks were walling off the information their users
posted [8], and RFC 9518 describes the general mechanism: functions
built on open protocols still centralize through network effects and
switching costs [9]. The consequences reach the public sphere:
Engagement-optimized ranking amplifies some political content over
other content [10], and the EU's Digital Services Act now obliges the
largest platforms to assess such systemic risks and to open their data
to vetted researchers [11], an obligation that remains hard to operationalize
because researchers must specify data whose existence they cannot
observe [12].<!-- was: [11], which has been shown to be defunct in principle [x]. --> The Commission's first fine under the Act, €120 million
against X in December 2025, concerned a "verified" badge that anyone
could buy without an identity check and terms that barred researchers
from public data [13]. The Web itself authenticates domains through
TLS certificates, but it offers no native way to state verifiably who
stands behind a document or an account, or what qualifies them [14]. The W3C now
lists among its Ethical Web Principles that the Web "makes it possible
to verify information", since "the public needs verifiable source and
context information to recognize trustworthy web publishers and
content" [15].
Even the data that platforms do release differs from what their users
see [16, 17]. Press publishers face the same opacity: in October 2026,
the Paris Court of Appeal upheld an order that Meta negotiate
neighbouring-rights payments with French media in good faith, finding
that Meta had supplied only fragmentary, opaque, and unverifiable data
on its use of their content [18]. Legal scholarship treats such architectural choices as
regulation in their own right [19, 20, 21]. And, currently, generative
AI repeats the pattern: answer engines and agentic browsers return
answers in place of links and so control access to the Web's content
[22], although the W3C Technical Architecture Group (TAG) holds that a
user agent acting on its user's behalf owes "meaningful visibility
into the sources it relied on" [23].<!-- was: [22], and the Court of Justice of the EU must now decide whether a
chatbot's summaries of press articles infringe the publisher's rights
[removed: n21]. (C-250/25 Like Company dropped 2026-10-30 for the decided Paris Meta case) --> Reddit
shows where this leads: after AI answers cut its search referrals by
more than a million per week, it announced in October 2026 a move to a
primarily logged-in experience, in part to protect its data from
scraping [24]. The properties that high-stakes communities need are thus
available today, but each inside one platform and under its control.

Society pays for these omissions: In 2007 the UK government found that
more than half of the roughly 4,000 URLs cited in ministerial
answers to Parliamentary Questions since 1997 no longer resolved, and<!-- was: URLs ministers had given in answers to Parliamentary Questions -->
built a redirect service into its national Web archive in response
[25]; one in five scholarly articles cites Web content that has since
rotted [26]. Two days before Slovakia's 2023 election, a fabricated
recording of a party leader discussing vote-rigging circulated with no
provenance that voters could check [27]. From February 2027, batteries
sold in the EU (and, soon after, products from clothes to detergents)
must carry a digital passport with carbon-footprint and due-diligence
data assembled from suppliers along the chain [28]. Law shows the cost
most sharply, because there a reference's stability is part of the
claim it supports. The EU has given judgments and legislation
persistent identifiers through ELI [29, 30], but these name documents,
not cited passages or versions; half the URLs cited in U.S. Supreme
Court opinions no longer resolve, and Perma.cc archives cited pages
one citation at a time [31]. By early 2026, more than 1,200 court
decisions worldwide concerned AI-fabricated citations [32], and the
High Court of England and Wales warned that citing fabricated
authorities may lead to referral to the regulator or to contempt
proceedings [33]. Content provenance is being moved into camera
hardware [34] and into EU law [35, 11], and the Munich Regional Court
held in November 2025 that a language model's memorization of song
lyrics infringes copyright [36], a similar question to what *The New
York Times v. OpenAI* is litigating in the U.S. [37, 38]. What these
cases have in common is that, in each case, coordination cost that the
Web avoided was deferred, not eliminated; and society now pays it one
incident at a time.

**The idea.** We propose to extend the Web’s architecture to build
selected properties into the Web itself, in a way that permits
communities that require them to select their own property
configuration, i.e., a set of desired *non-functional properties*
(NFPs). ISLANDS introduces the *island* to capture such configured
spaces: *Islands* are bounded networked information spaces, such as a
legal-citation community, a supply chain, or a community of
public-interest publishers on social media, whose members share stakes
high enough to justify the cost of coordination through an ex-ante
selected architectural trade-off rather than ex-post patching or
litigation. Each island selects the NFPs its stakes demand and
provides them jointly within its scope, while islands interoperate
with one another and with the open Web through the Web's existing
low-coordination mechanisms that permit planetary scaling. Within an
island, references can for instance be defined as span-level,
version-pinned, bidirectional, and verifiable, similar to how Xanadu
specified them [39, 40]; across the boundary, they degrade to ordinary
Web links, so that every island remains backwards-compatible with
today's Web. Islands can also move away from the Web in the opposite
direction: an island of fixed, high-speed installations may bind its
interfaces once, at design time, in a binary encoding, much as a
linker resolves a program's symbols when it builds the program [37,
38]. Entering an island is an explicit admission step: a client, human
or agent, is shown the island's NFP set and accepts the obligations of
membership, and this is where the island applies its attribution
regime. Through ISLANDS, we further explore NFPs that have not yet
been proposed on an architectural level. These include
*c**onfidentiality-preserving references* that resolve to a value that
members can verify was correctly derived from data they may not read;
this generalizes *secure multi-party computation* (SMPC), which has run
in production since 2008 [41, 42]. They also include *p**urpose
binding* which attaches the terms of a disclosure (recipient, purpose,
validity window) to the reference, so that a reference is re-resolved
when its terms expire [43]. *Attribution* binds a reference to an
accountable author or, through anonymous credentials and the
pseudonyms the European Digital Identity Wallet must support, to an
eligible but unidentified one [44, 45, 46]. *Verifiable credentials*
let a reference carry an issuer's attested claim about its source,
e.g., that a filing was signed by a member of the bar or that an
emissions figure was checked by an accredited verifier [14, 47].
*Capability discovery* shows agents, after each interaction, only the
actions currently valid, in the hypermedia style of REST [48] as
extended to multi-agent systems [49, 50, 51]; current LLM agents
instead receive statically linked tool catalogues [52, 53]. The
unifying scientific contribution is a *theory of chosen guarantees*:
a formal account of which NFP bundles an island can provide, at which
coordination cost, under which threat model, and for which
stakeholders. Neither hypertext research nor Web engineering offers
this today (b.2).

### b.2 State of knowledge and the gap (~0.75 page)

<!-- was: b.2 before the PI restructuring of 2026-10-30 (11:25 save); full text in draft/archive/B1-b1-before-PI-edit-2026-10-29.md. PI notes resolved: date (mid-1990s, Kahle 1997), Xanadu ref, three transitions, gap paragraph. -->
Hypertext research traditionally assumed rich, span-anchored links
(e.g., [39, 54, 55]), and its agenda included versioning and computed
structure (e.g., [56]): a link would persist; it would point at a
specific range of bytes rather than a full page; and modifications of
the link would lead to the creation of a new version rather than the
replacement of the link. Open-hypermedia systems in addition
implemented *bidirectional* links and *referential integrity* at
workgroup scale [57, 58]; link rot had thus been addressed even before
the Web made it endemic. The Web dropped these guarantees in order to
scale [1, 48], and this was a sensible and pragmatic choice at the
time.

Since the mid-1990s, when the Internet Archive began to archive the
Web [59], remedial infrastructure (e.g., persistent identifier
overlays, Web archives, Memento [60], Perma.cc [31], and robust
hyperlinks [61]) has kept this architecture and compensated for its
drawbacks, such as link rot and misattribution, e.g., through Web
archives with permanent links such as the *Internet Archive's Wayback
Machine* or, in the research space, *Zenodo* [62] (since link rot
undermines reproducibility [26]). Such compensation did not touch the
architectural principles of the Web, since changing them might
fragment what the W3C's Ethical Web Principles call "one web" [15].
W3C specifications address passages and backlinks on the open Web:
*Web Annotation* selectors [63] and *text fragments* [64] identify a
passage of a page, and *Webmention* [65] and *Linked Data
Notifications* [66], which the W3C *Social Web Working Group*
maintains together with *ActivityPub* [67], deliver opt-in backlinks.
None of them, however, pins a version or verifies what it points to,
since this would induce architectural change with undesired side
effects (e.g., it would undermine scalability of the Web). Dedicated
services assemble specific NFP profiles as well, e.g., persistence via
*Perma.cc* or *Zenodo*, provenance via *C2PA* [34], and identity and
notarization via *DOIs* or *blockchains*. *Solid* [68] brings *data
sovereignty*, since users keep their data in personal stores of their
choice; *privacy and access control*, since each application reads
only what the user grants it; and *portability*, since the data is
decoupled from individual applications. These developments are
evidence of the demand for chosen guarantees that extend beyond those
of the basic Web architecture. However, each is an individual remedy
that provides one fixed profile, and they do not touch the
architectural foundation of the Web.

Today, a widely applicable architectural shift is possible, based on
methods that were not available in the early days of the Web:
*Content-addressed* and *versioned storage* (Git; IPFS [69]; Software
Heritage identifiers [70]) and *provenance attestation* [34, 71] now
supply, at Web scale, the substrate that Xanadu [39, 40] lacked. They
are, however, storage and signing primitives without span semantics, a
link layer, or a governance model. *Nanopublications* [72, 73] form a
deployed, federated network of immutable, signed semantic statements
with self-verifying identifiers; they do not, however, offer span
addressing over arbitrary content, *transclusion*, or
confidentiality-preserving resolution; from the perspective of this
proposal, nanopublications may become an *island* at statement
granularity.

Today’s re-decentralization efforts relocate *control* and leave
*addressability* as it was. Solid's pods keep location-addressed,
mutable resources [68], and data spaces relocate contracts and
governance [74]. Federated social media behaves similarly:
*ActivityPub* distributes servers but keeps posts location-addressed
and mutable [67], and Bluesky's *AT Protocol* signs and
content-addresses each user's repository [75] but leaves references
between posts unverified and offers one fixed set of guarantees to all
communities. Switching into the regulatory space, EU data law follows
the same line as well: the *Data Act* and the portability rights of
the *GDPR* and the *DMA* move *copies of data* between holders [76],
and the Solid deployments that implement them (cf. our own [77, 78])
leave references location-addressed. However, raw-data transfer cannot
sustain a market, hence we have recently seen proposals of trading
*purpose-bound data derivations* whose terms limit their shelf life
and use [43]. There is today no reference layer that could name such a
derivation, pin it to a version, or carry its terms. *Usage control*
formalized such terms [79, 80], and *sticky policies* bind them to the
data [81]. Enforcement after release, however, requires a trusted
monitor at the recipient or falls back on detection after the fact
[80]. RFC 9518 explains why open specifications alone do not prevent
centralization, and we adopt the design-level mitigations it lists
[9]. It is relevant to note that network architecture faced the same
question one layer below. Clark et al. argued that an Internet
embedded in society must leave room for the *tussle* between
stakeholders with conflicting interests, and proposed design for
change and controlled transparency as its tenets [82, 83]. Plutarch
argued for heterogeneous network contexts joined by translating
functions at their borders [84]. These positions state principles on
the network layer that mirror the issues we highlight on the
application layer of the World Wide Web, but they concern packets and
addresses, not references.

The NFPs discussed so far concern what a reference points to. A second
group concerns who may see, trust, and act on it, and here, too, the
building blocks exist but are not part of the Web's architecture.
Confidentiality comes first: communities need results computed over
data that no single member may disclose. Secure multi-party
computation (SMPC) has run in production since the Danish sugar-beet
auction of 2008 [41] and in Estonian government statistics [42], and
general-purpose protocols and implementations such as SPDZ [85] and
MP-SPDZ [86] make arbitrary computations practical. Our PEP-LCA
protocol computes joint supply-chain assessments across up to 50,000
participants without a trusted third party [87], and a follow-on
system anchors share disclosure in Solid's access-control layer [88].
Again, each of these deployments is, however, an individual
application, and none exposes its result as a reference that others
can cite and re-resolve. Confidentiality keeps data from the parties
that use it; trust needs the converse, a verifiable statement about
who stands behind the data. Verifiable credentials address this part
of the problem: the W3C data model [14], selective-disclosure formats
such as SD-JWT [89], and the European Digital Identity Wallet [46] let
a holder prove an issuer's claim (e.g., a professional license)
without revealing more than needed [47, 90]. They are, however,
presented to one verifier within one session, and they are not bound
to the references that the claim concerns. Credentials tell a client
whom it deals with; autonomous agents also need to know what they may
do. Hypermedia multi-agent systems research argues for run-time
*affordance* discovery [49, 91]. Current LLM-agent practice, in
contrast, uses statically linked tool lists [52, 53], which indirect
prompt injection turns into an attack surface [92], and no existing
design connects *capability discovery* to a verifiable reference
layer.

As a community, we have thus built imperfect remedies instead of an
architected approach. The reason is that an architected approach is
hard: it requires consolidating fields that have so far advanced
separately, namely hypertext, distributed systems, cryptography,
multi-agent systems, and law.

**The gap.** We are not aware of any system that composes
immutability, span-level verifiable addressing, transclusion,
confidentiality-preserving resolution, and agent-facing affordance
discovery at the architectural level. Closing this gap requires an
understanding of each of these domains, spanning distributed
computing, decentralization, and the societal demands behind them. It
must also be done in a backwards-compatible way that reuses the Web's
architecture, and its NFPs, as deployed today. Finally, any such
architecture needs a theory that predicts which NFP bundles fit a
given deployment context and community.

### b.3 Objectives (~0.5 page)

<!-- was: b.3 and b.4 before the WP restructuring of 2026-10-31 (PI request: WP1 islands, WP2 link classes, WP3 border checks); full text in draft/archive/B1-b3-b4-before-WP-restructure-2026-10-31.md -->

The goal of ISLANDS is to find out which guarantees a Web
architecture can provide to the communities that need them, and at
what cost, and to demonstrate these guarantees in practice. This
involves:

- **O1 — Societally required islands.** We establish which *islands*,
  i.e., which bundles of NFPs, society requires today. We start from
  four drafts grounded in the PI's work: legal citation and copyright
  (Island L), industrial supply chains (Island S), platforms,
  misinformation, and manipulation (Island M), and fixed installations
  that trade the Web's uniform interface for binary interfaces
  (Island F). An *architectural decision record* states which islands
  the project does not pursue.
- **O2 — Theory of chosen guarantees.** We isolate the *link classes*
  that the required islands need and develop a formal model that
  relates them to coordination *and economic* costs, island size,
  threat models, and stakeholder incentives. The taxonomy has a
  persistence and integrity ladder (*Classic Jumplinks*, *Archived*,
  *Trusted*, *Verified*, and *Cryptographic Links*, in increasing
  strength) and five cross-cutting axes (attribution, trust, usage,
  modality, *link time*). For each class, we prove what it minimally
  requires, and we derive decision procedures that an island designer
  can apply.
- **O3 — Border translation.** We determine how NFPs, and the concepts
  that support them (versions, identities, credentials, usage terms,
  encodings), are translated when information enters or leaves an
  island: which references are upgraded at admission, which are
  downgraded on exit, and how both happen without the silent loss of a
  guarantee.
- **O4 — Island technologies.** We build the technologies that the
  required islands need: a reference and *transclusion* layer over
  content-addressed storage; confidential references that turn
  SMPC-based resolution into an addressing primitive; and an agent
  layer with state-dependent *capability discovery* and design-time
  *Static Links*.
- **O5 — Validation and incentives.** We instantiate and evaluate
  Islands L, S, and M with their communities and Island F in industrial
  installations, and we measure whether the islands change each actor's

### b.4 Research strategy and methods (~0.5 page)

<!-- was: detailed work-package descriptions (about 2,600 words), replaced 2026-10-31 at PI request by a half-page overview of the final WPs (placeholder text for the PI to rework); full text and the reference list of that version in draft/archive/B1-b4-detailed-WPs-2026-10-31.md; the detailed methods are in B2 Section 2 -->
The work packages follow the order of the argument: WP1 establishes
the islands that society requires, WP2 and WP3 derive in parallel what
these islands need inside and at their borders, WP4 builds the
technologies, and WP5 validates them with their communities.

**WP1 — Societally required islands (M1–M18, revised annually).**
Starting from the four drafts in Figure 1, we determine with the
actors who would run and use them which NFP bundles society requires:
in law and copyright (Island L), industrial supply chains (Island S),
platforms and the public sphere (Island M), and fixed installations
that deliberately downgrade the uniform interface to binary bindings
(Island F). Interviews with each class of actor, the RFC 9518
checklist [9], and the W3C TAG's societal-impact self-review [93]
test each draft. WP1 delivers an account of the required islands and
an *architectural decision record* of the islands that ISLANDS does
not pursue.

**WP2 — Link classes and the theory of chosen guarantees (M6–M60).**
WP2 isolates the *link classes* that these islands need, from *Classic
Jumplinks* to *Cryptographic Links*, with cross-cutting axes for
attribution, trust, usage, modality, and *link time*, and formalizes
them as a *guarantee calculus* in the information-flow control
tradition. Each class carries two proof obligations, *realizability*
and *necessity*, so that the calculus predicts which classes an island
of given size, stake, and threat model can sustain, and at what cost.

**WP3 — Border checks (M6–M48, in parallel with WP2).** WP3 determines
how NFPs, and the concepts that support them, are translated when
information enters or leaves an island: which references are upgraded
at admission, which are downgraded on exit, and which cannot cross.
Its governing rule is that no reference loses a guarantee silently.
WP3 delivers a translation table, an *admission protocol* based on
verifiable credentials [14], and border gateways for each island.

**WP4 — Island technologies (M12–M54).** From the islands and their
link classes, we build the technologies they require: a reference and
*transclusion* layer over content-addressed storage (T4.1),
confidential references that generalize our SMPC system [87, 88] into
an addressing primitive (T4.2), and an agent layer with
state-dependent *capability discovery* and design-time *Static Links*
(T4.3).

**WP5 — Validation islands and incentives (M18–M60).** We instantiate
Islands L, S, and M with their communities and demonstrate Island F in
industrial installations, measure which guarantees each island holds
and at what cost, and repeat the WP1 interviews to test whether the
islands changed each actor's incentive to centralize.

**Integration and openness.** Annual integration milestones bind the
prototypes into one architecture and revise the WP1 account and the
calculus. All software and publications are open, and all
specifications go to the W3C groups that maintain the protocols
islands build on.
