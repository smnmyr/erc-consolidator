### b.1 The problem and the idea (~1.5 pages)

<!-- was (before porting the PI restructuring in B1.docx, 2026-11-11): The World Wide Web's original design favored simplicity, decentralization, and low coordination overhead, which enabled explosive adoption but sacrificed stable references, built-in versioning, and native bidirectional linking. Generative AI now repeats the pattern: answer engines and agentic browsers return answers in place of links and so control access to the Web's content [17]. The W3C Technical Architecture Group (TAG), in contrast, holds that a user agent acting on its user's behalf owes "meaningful visibility into the sources it relied on" [18]. Highlighting real-world consequences, Reddit announced in
October 2026 a move to a primarily logged-in experience, to protect
its data from scraping [19] after AI answers cut its search referrals
by more than a million per week. This shows that, today, the
properties that high-stakes communities require are available, but
inside closed platforms and under these platforms’ control – hardly an
Open Web.

Society pays for these omissions: In 2007, the UK government found that more than half of the roughly 4,000 URLs cited in ministerial answers to Parliamentary Questions since 1997 no longer resolved, and it built a redirect service into its national Web archive in response [20]. One in five scholarly articles cites Web content that has since rotted [21]. Two days before Slovakia's 2023 election, a fabricated recording
of a party leader discussing vote-rigging circulated with no
provenance that voters could check [23]. From February 2027, batteries
sold in the EU (and, soon after, products from clothes to detergents)
must carry a digital passport with carbon-footprint and due-diligence

**Why now.** Building these properties into the architecture pays
off today, where it did not in 1989. First, the coordination they
require has become far less costly. Content addressing [48],
*verifiable credentials*, a W3C Recommendation since 2025 [42], and
practical *secure multi-party computation* [49] are standardized and
deployed, and every EU Member State must offer a European Digital
Identity Wallet by 24 December 2026 [41]. Second, demand for the same
guarantees now arrives from many directions at once. The DSA requires
attribution and research access [12], the Data Act portability [50], the AI Act respect for machine-readable reservations against text and data mining [35], and the battery passport and CBAM verifiable supply-chain data
[24, 51]. Today, every platform implements each of these
obligations separately, and every regulator checks them separately:
N platforms and M obligations yield N × M integrations. An
architecture specifies each obligation once, as an NFP bundle, and
lets each platform implement it once, which turns N × M into N + M.
Third, platform-level solutions fail visibly. A platform can withdraw
or silently change what it offers, as the cases above show, and
machines, which now produce most Web traffic [52], cannot check a
platform's promises. Finally, the protocols that will carry these
guarantees are being standardized now, in the W3C groups on the
social Web, storage, credentials, and agents and in agent protocols
such as MCP [55] and x402 [56]. Once they settle, the opportunity to
build the guarantees in closes, as it did in the 1990s.

**The idea.** ISLANDS proposes to *extend* today’s Web’s architecture and build selected properties into the Web itself. Communities that require these properties can then select their *own property configuration*, i.e., a set of desired *non-functional properties* (NFPs) that apply to their part of the Web. Such communities are not small. One example is the *copyright community*, i.e., all stakeholders with an interest in having content rights preserved by the architecture, from authors and publishers to collecting societies and courts. Another is an industry's supply chain that must account for its carbon emissions. *Islands* are bounded networked information spaces, such as a legal-citation community, a supply chain that accounts for its carbon emissions, or a community of public-interest publishers on social media. Their members share stakes high enough to justify the cost of coordination, and they settle it through an ex-ante selected architectural trade-off rather than ex-post patching or litigation. *The New York Times*, for instance, is litigating the use of its articles by AI models [33]. It could choose to publish its articles into an island whose NFPs bind every quotation to its source through traceable *transclusion* and carry the paper's reservation against text and data mining [34, 35] in each reference.
Readers and AI models that accept these terms enter the island. A
paywall achieves less: it controls access on one platform, and once an
article has been read, its quotations and copies leave without terms
or provenance. In the island, the terms and the attribution travel
with every quotation to every participating site and agent, and reuse
becomes verifiable without any single platform controlling the
articles. Each island selects the NFPs its stakes demand and
provides them jointly within its scope, while islands interoperate
with one another and with the open Web through the Web's existing
low-coordination mechanisms that permit planetary scaling. Within an island, references can for instance be span-level, version-pinned, bidirectional, and verifiable, similar to how Xanadu specified them [37, removed: n43]. Across the boundary, they degrade to ordinary Web links, so that every island remains backwards-compatible with today's Web. Islands can also move away from the Web in the opposite direction. An island of fixed, high-speed installations may bind its interfaces once, at design time, in a binary encoding, much as a linker resolves a program's symbols when it builds the program [29, 30]. Entering an island is an explicit admission step: a client, human
or agent, is shown the island's NFP set and accepts the obligations of
membership, and this is where the island applies its attribution
regime. Through ISLANDS, we further explore NFPs that have not yet
been proposed on an architectural level. These include
*confidentiality-preserving references* that resolve to a value that
members can verify was correctly derived from data they may not read;
this generalizes *secure multi-party computation* (SMPC), which has
run in production since 2008 [39, removed: n46]. They also include *purpose
binding* which attaches the terms of a disclosure (recipient, purpose,
validity window) to the reference, so that a reference is re-resolved
when its terms expire [39]. *Attribution* binds a reference to an
accountable author or, through anonymous credentials and the
pseudonyms the European Digital Identity Wallet must support, to an
eligible but unidentified one [removed: n48, 41, 42]. *Verifiable credentials*
let a reference carry an issuer's attested claim about its source,
e.g., that a filing was signed by a member of the bar or that an
emissions figure was checked by an accredited verifier [43, removed: n52].
*Capability discovery* shows agents, after each interaction, only the
actions currently valid, in the hypermedia style of REST [2] as
extended to multi-agent systems [44, removed: n54, 45]; current LLM agents
instead receive statically linked tool catalogues [55, removed: n98]. The
unifying scientific contribution is a *theory of chosen guarantees*: a
formal account of which NFP bundles an island can provide, at which
coordination cost, under which threat model, and for which
stakeholders. Neither hypertext research nor Web engineering offers
this today (b.2).

Figure 1 shows five example islands within the Web. Each island is a part of the Web's network whose clients and servers raise the contract that the Web offers with the NFPs that their community requires. As an example, *Island L* lets a court or author cite a passage that resolves to exactly the version relied on, and it makes a fabricated citation fail visibly. It leaves a trail of reuse that a copyright dispute, or any scheme that remunerates reuse, can follow, and it makes terms such as a rightholder's reservation against text and data mining [34, 35] verifiable in the reference. It does not, however, establish that a cited source is correct, and it records no reuse that happens outside the island. *Island S* lets suppliers report
confidential emission figures that can be checked without decryption;
*Island M* lets readers see who stands behind an account and how a
post was edited or deleted, and lets researchers obtain exposure
statistics; *Island N* keeps signed, content-hashed research claims, as published in the deployed nanopublication network [removed: n57, 47, 48], citable and attributed. It ties each claim through span addressing to the specific passage of the source on which it rests. *Island F*, finally, lets fixed installations interact over binary interfaces at low latency while giving up evolvability.

decentralization, and low coordination overhead, which enabled
explosive adoption but sacrificed stable references, built-in
versioning, and native bidirectional linking [1, 2]. This was a
deliberate choice: Berners-Lee's 1989 proposal required that the

*sovereignty* over their data, and society requires *trusted* content
and *accountable* authorship. These needs should be satisfied
*architecturally*, but in a way that is backwards-compatible with

As a community, we have thus built imperfect remedies instead of an
architected approach. The reason is that an architected approach is
hard: it requires consolidating fields that have so far advanced
separately, namely hypertext, distributed systems, cryptography,
multi-agent systems, and law.

The methods that make such a shift possible today (Section 1) were not available in the early days of the Web: *Content-addressed* and *versioned storage* (Git; IPFS
[48]; Software Heritage identifiers [77]) and *provenance attestation*

part of the Web's architecture. With respect to confidentiality,
communities need results computed over data that no single member may
disclose. Secure multi-party computation (SMPC) has run in production
since 2008 [38] and general-purpose protocols and implementations such
as SPDZ [79] and MP-SPDZ [49] make computation practical. Our PEP-LCA
protocol computes joint supply-chain assessments across up to 50,000
participants without a trusted third party [80], and a follow-on
system anchors share disclosure in Solid's access-control layer [81].
Again, each of these deployments is, however, an individual
application, and none exposes its result as a reference that others
can cite and re-resolve. Confidentiality keeps data from the parties
that use it; trust needs the converse, a verifiable statement about
who stands behind the data. Verifiable credentials address this part of the problem. The W3C data model [42], selective-disclosure formats such as SD-JWT [82], and the European Digital Identity Wallet [41] let a holder prove an issuer's claim (e.g., a professional license) without revealing more than needed [removed: n52, removed: n96]. They are, however,
presented to one verifier within one session, and they are not bound
to the references that the claim concerns. Credentials tell a client
whom it deals with; autonomous agents also need to know what they may
do. Hypermedia multi-agent systems research argues for run-time
*affordance* discovery [43, 83]. Current LLM-agent practice, in
contrast, uses statically linked tool lists [55, removed: n98], which indirect
prompt injection turns into an attack surface [removed: n98], and no existing
design connects *capability discovery* to a verifiable reference
layer.

**What we do not know.** Today, we do not know how different NFP
selections work together. Each hypertext system and each remedy above
fixes one bundle of guarantees by design. None of them tells us which
bundles are technically feasible in the first place, at what
coordination cost, and against which threat model. It is open, for
instance, whether *confidentiality-preserving references* can be
combined with bidirectional backlinks without the backlinks revealing
what the references hide, or whether attribution and anonymity can
coexist in one island. This is a major gap. O1 and WP1 determine which
bundles society requires, and O2 and WP2 determine which of them are
attainable, at what cost, and under which threat model.

**The gap.** We are not aware of any system that composes immutability,
span-level verifiable addressing, transclusion,
confidentiality-preserving resolution, and agent-facing affordance
discovery *at the architectural level*. Closing this gap requires an

Hypertext research traditionally assumed rich, span-anchored links (e.g., [36, 57]), and its agenda included versioning and computed structure (e.g., [1]). A link would *persist*, and it would point at a *specific range of bytes* rather than a full page. Modifying a link would create a new version instead of replacing the link. Open-hypermedia systems implemented *bidirectional* links and *referential integrity* at workgroup scale [58, 59]; link rot had thus been addressed even before the Web made it endemic. The Web dropped these guarantees to scale [3, 4], and this was a sensible and pragmatic choice at the time.

ISLANDS tests whether a coordination cost that was judged prohibitive
when the Web’s design was established [3, 4] is sustainable at
*island* scale with today’s technologies and approaches. It also tests
individual aspects of this problem, such as whether cryptographic
reference resolution can reach interactive latency in a realistic

adopt shared guarantees. Finally, it tests whether, for the
communities that need them, guarantees built into the architecture
cost society less than today's platform-level fixes. These are risky

founding trade-off now fails at measurable cost, with large

regulated confidential industrial data. ISLANDS furthermore considers
autonomous agents acting on the Web’s hypermedia fabric from the
beginning, building on our work on hypermedia multi-agent systems [43, 83, 44]. If our hypotheses fail, our theory of chosen guarantees
still delimits which guarantees are attainable, for whom, and at what
cost.

The alternative is to continue as today, with piecemeal fixes whose
costs society absorbs one incident at a time. Where this might lead is
sketched, in exaggerated form, by the *Dead Internet Theory*, which
holds that most online content and activity is already produced by
bots [84]. We do not endorse the theory fully, but its premise is
moving closer: automated traffic surpassed human traffic on the Web in
2024 [52], and language models degrade when trained on content that
models generated [85]. A Web that cannot state who stands behind a
document, which version a claim rests on, or whether a text was
generated, becomes a space in which neither people nor machines can
rely on what they read. Courts, parliaments, and markets would then
have to rebuild trust outside the Web, at far greater cost than the
coordination the Web avoided. ISLANDS offers the architectural
alternative: guarantees that communities choose and that hold where
they are needed.

### Application note: Island L and Open Terms Archive

Open Terms Archive (opentermsarchive.org) records every version of the
terms of service and privacy policies of digital services "to enable
democratic oversight", and the European Commission's DSA enforcement
team uses it. Contributors declare, per document, a URL and CSS
selectors; robots fetch each document several times a day, store the
source as a snapshot, filter it into a textual version, and commit
dated versions to public Git repositories, which collections publish
as datasets and through a federated API. Open Terms Archive also informs the European Commission about changes in the terms it tracks.<!-- was: as datasets and through a federated API. --> Terms published in Island L
would make most of this machinery unnecessary. A provider would
publish each version of its terms as an immutable, signed record and
each change as a superseding record, so that polling robots, change detection, and the dated commits that a third party makes on the provider's behalf would no longer be needed. The Commission would no longer depend on a third party to learn of a change either: it could subscribe to the provider's terms through *Live/Subscription Links* and be notified as soon as the provider publishes a superseding version, which the provider has signed and cannot later deny.<!-- was: so that polling robots, change detection, and the dated commits that a third party makes on the provider's behalf would no longer be needed. --> Declarations with
selectors and filters, and their maintenance whenever a provider
redesigns its pages, would also become unnecessary, since the island
holds the text itself and not a rendered page. Authenticity would
rest on the provider's signature, which a regulator or court can
check, instead of on trust in the archivist's crawl. Span addressing
would add what the archive lacks today: a verifiable reference to the
exact clause of the exact version that a user accepted, which a
consumer, regulator, or court can cite. Open Terms Archive would remain
necessary for providers that do not join the island, and those that
benefit most from opaque terms are the least likely to join. Its collections, analyses, and federated search would remain valuable in any case, as would its memos that explain to the Commission what a change means, but they would work on verified records instead of crawled copies.<!-- was: Its collections, analyses, and federated search would remain valuable in any case, but would work on verified records instead of crawled copies. -->

superseding version exists. This answers the question that the
investigation leaves open under Implementing Regulation (EU)

rest on obsolete data. Island L would neither prevent a platform from
publishing wrong or inconsistent figures, nor make a correction
visible if the platform publishes outside the island.
